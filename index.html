What FSD Supervised is

Full Self-Driving (Supervised), opens in a new window is Tesla’s most capable driver-assistance system. It can steer, accelerate, brake, change lanes, handle junctions and park. The driver supervises. It is SAE Level 2. It is not an Automated Driving System, not a robotaxi, and not unsupervised. Every official European text that matters — RDW, Færdselsstyrelsen, Transpordiamet, Traficom, the French transport minister — says the driver remains responsible. See what it is, and is not.

In Europe the approved customer product is Hardware 4 / AI4 only. Check the car, not the model year: Controls → Software → Additional Vehicle Information should show AI4 or HW4. Older Hardware 3 cars are not on the Dutch type-approval. An over-the-air update will not unlock FSD on a Hardware 3 car in the EU. See Hardware 4 only.

The software is a separate approved build. European cars have been on a frozen v14.2.2.x branch. North America has moved on to v14.3.x. RDW said it plainly on 10 April 2026: the US version is “NOT comparable” to the EU version.

Where it is legal, owners subscribe in the Tesla app. After Tesla withdrew the European one-time purchase in May 2026, the published offer is a monthly subscription — commonly €99 in euro markets, often €49 for owners who previously bought Enhanced Autopilot. Confirm the live price in the app. Hands need not stay on the wheel, but they must be able to take over at once.

Why this matters

Around 19,400 people were killed on EU roads in 2025, according to the European Commission’s preliminary figures published on 24 March 2026, opens in a new window — about 3% fewer than in 2024. The Commission’s working estimate is that around 100,000 people are seriously injured on EU roads each year. Most Member States are not on track to halve deaths by 2030. Driver assistance will not solve that on its own. The remaining question is whether Member States finish the review of this file and take a public position.

Tesla’s 4.1× (1 September 2026) is a company figure across 100 million km on public EU roads. Unlike the July 5.2× slide, that post does not say it is a same-fleet comparison against Teslas with the same Active Safety suite. Tesla also published an Article 39 evidence dashboard, opens in a new window it says was shared with Member States in April 2026. The data on that dashboard is from before that date and is mostly North American customer fleet plus a small EU engineering fleet, not the 100 million km of customer use. Tesla’s own methodology note says it cannot control for weather, time of day, traffic density, or which trips get FSD. It is still Tesla’s collision definition, Tesla’s baseline, and Tesla’s arithmetic. RDW did its own tests and does not treat Tesla marketing as the basis of approval. There is no public, independent, peer-reviewed European collision study of customer FSD Supervised as of 1 September 2026. These figures are a reason to finish the review. They are not a reason to skip it.

Questions

Full Self-Driving (Supervised), opens in a new window is Tesla’s advanced driver-assistance system. It uses eight exterior cameras and a neural net trained on fleet driving. It can navigate city streets, junctions, lane changes and parking while the driver supervises.

It is SAE Level 2. It is not Level 3, not unsupervised FSD, and not a robotaxi. The name is Tesla’s. The legal class in Europe is a Driver Control Assistance System, sitting under UN Regulation No. 171 with exemptions. The driver is responsible for the vehicle at every moment.

It is not “banned in Europe.” It is a paid product in five Member States, on a specific hardware generation and a specific software build.

As of 1 September 2026, customer use in Europe is legal in five Member States: the Netherlands, Lithuania, Estonia, Denmark and Belgium. Hardware 4 only. See Where FSD Supervised is legal for the live map and every national status.

Outside Europe, Tesla’s late-August 2026 list named twelve markets: the United States, Canada, Mexico, Puerto Rico, Australia, New Zealand, South Korea, and those five EU countries. China is not on that August list. Confirm the live list on tesla.com/fsd, opens in a new window. Software is not the same everywhere: North America has been on v14.3.x; the approved EU customer build is v14.2.2.x.

Europe was late because UN Regulation No. 171 (DCAS), which entered into force on 22 September 2024, does not cover everything this product does. Tesla therefore needed an exemption as well as a DCAS home. That exemption is Article 39 of Regulation (EU) 2018/858, opens in a new window.

There are now three layers, and they are not the same thing:

  1. National recognition (Article 39(5)). RDW issued a provisional EU type-approval, valid in the Netherlands. Any other Member State may accept that file on its own territory. Four have. The rest have not.
  2. TCMV / Commission authorisation (Article 39(3)). The Commission must authorise an EU-wide implementing act. TCMV votes by qualified majority: 15 Member States and 65% of EU population. A vote is widely expected in October 2026. It is not on a published Commission agenda as of 27 August 2026.
  3. UN R171 Series 02 — the longer, cleaner path. If adopted, manufacturers can apply for a standard UN certificate. That is the durable path for the UK, Switzerland and Norway. It is not a shortcut around today’s Article 39 vote.

If the Commission later refuses the Article 39 authorisation, the five national approvals can be unwound. That rule is in What if the Commission says no?.

The European customer approval is latest-generation hardware only — Hardware 4 / AI4, sold roughly from 2023. Traficom said so officially, opens in a new window on 23 June 2026: for older cars, “the manufacturer has not initiated a corresponding procedure.” Hardware 3 FSD v14 Lite is a US (and, narrowly, Korean) product. It is not the approved EU type.

The EU software is a frozen approved build on the v14.2.2 branch. North America is on v14.3.x. Each safety-relevant EU build needs type-approval follow-up. Do not assume feature parity, and do not tell a 2019–2023 Hardware 3 owner that a simple OTA will unlock it.

How to check: Controls → Software → Additional Vehicle Information should show AI4 or HW4. Build dates are a clue, not a proof: mid-2023+ S/X, early-2024+ 3/Y.

This is the live political objection. Traficom describes a driver-set speed offset of up to +50% above the detected limit, plus a “contextual” speed that can follow surrounding traffic. Tesla’s stated justification is wrongly read speed limits, and not becoming a rolling obstacle. That has not convinced Paris. Stockholm’s technical letter is still on file.

France. On 22 July 2026, transport minister Philippe Tabarot declined national recognition of the current product. Le Figaro reported him saying the safety trade-offs were not yet sufficient “as it currently stands,” and that France wants a harmonised EU framework rather than a national copy of the Dutch file.

Sweden. Trafikverket’s 30 April 2026 TCMV letter, reported by Reuters on 18 June, recommends a no unless Speed Offset / systematic speeding is removed. Infrastrukturminister Andreas Carlson’s written answer to riksdagsfråga 2025/26:1000 (letter 31 August, published 1 September 2026) restates a union-wide ADAS ambition and still takes Trafikverket into account. That is not a locked TCMV yes, not a withdrawal of the Speed Offset objection, and not national customer approval. Testing is not a go-ahead.

A letter from France should address the 22 July refusal: either ask for a yes with conditions, or ask the ministry to say what change would turn a no into a yes.

A letter from Sweden must not treat Carlson’s written answer as a locked yes. Ask for a public TCMV line while Trafikverket’s April letter is still on file.

Then the provisional Dutch approval — and every national recognition that hangs off it — can lapse six months after the refusal (Article 39(7); Færdselsstyrelsen, 9 June 2026). New marketing and sale in the EU would stop. Cars already built to the provisional approval and already in service in accepting states sit in a greyer residual category.

A refusal does not forbid a later, narrower file, or a later file under a revised UN R171. It does mean the current five-country map is not permanent until the Commission act exists. That is why the letters ask for a yes at TCMV, not only for a local favour.

European streets are denser than a US grid: narrower roads, trams, scooters, unmarked junctions, aggressive lane discipline. Tesla has been testing on those streets for more than a year. Since April 2026, customers have been driving the approved build in five countries. That is a stronger fact than any demo.

The driver is legally responsible. Always. FSD Supervised is SAE Level 2 (SAE J3016, opens in a new window). It is not an Automated Driving System under UN R157.

RDW’s own words: the vehicle is “NOT autonomous or self-driving. The driver is supported and remains responsible.” Hands need not stay on the wheel, but must be able to take over at once. Inattention leads to warnings and, if it continues, a temporary lock-out.

The cabin camera watches gaze and attention. The driver can cancel at any time with the brake, the wheel, or the stalk. FSD assists. The driver decides. Liability sits with the driver, as it does with adaptive cruise or lane-keeping.

No. Not legally, and not technically if the driver-monitoring system is working. Looking away, closing your eyes, or picking up a phone produces warnings and then a disengagement. Doing those things in a moving car remains an offence whether FSD is on or not.

Supervised assistance can reduce the grind of a long motorway or a jam. It does not buy you the right to do something else. Unsupervised / “texting while driving” talk is about a different product that is not what Article 39 authorised.

If you live in NL, LT, EE, DK or BE and the car is HW4: open the Tesla app and subscribe. Typical published price: €99 / month, often €49 if you already bought Enhanced Autopilot. Confirm in the app.

If you live elsewhere in Europe:

  • Write to your authority. That is the action that changes your roads.
  • Passenger ride-alongs. Tesla has offered supervised passenger-seat demos in countries that have not approved customer use. A Tesla employee sits in the driver’s seat. This is not customer FSD. Check tesla.com/events, opens in a new window.
  • Drive it yourself abroad in a compatible HW4 car in the five approved EU countries, or in the United States, Canada, Mexico, Australia, New Zealand or South Korea. Turo remains a way to rent in those markets; confirm FSD and hardware with the host.

Always supervise. Always Hardware 4 if you want the current EU-equivalent experience.

They are not in Article 39. They cannot recognise the Dutch provisional EU type-approval.

  • United Kingdom. Separate GB type-approval (VCA / DfT). Tesla’s UK site has said FSD is not yet available. UN R171 Series 02 is the realistic treaty path, plus GB traffic law.
  • Switzerland. A 1958 Agreement party that applies R171. FEDRO / ASTRA cannot accept the Dutch Article 39 file. Ride-alongs in Swiss cities are not an approval.
  • Norway. EEA, not Article 39. The NPRA has raised Speed Offset and winter-road concerns. Testing is not approval.

A later standard UN certificate under R171 Series 02 would be the durable route for all three. That is the longer path, not this autumn’s TCMV vote.
